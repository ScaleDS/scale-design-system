Motion

Motion is how the interface explains itself, and how the brand behaves. Get the first right and the product is easy to follow. Get both right and it feels alive.

Duration and Easing Transitions Choreography

Duration

How long a movement takes. Fast enough to feel like a reply, slow enough to follow: under about 100ms a change reads as instant, and past half a second it's stopped feeling like movement and started feeling like waiting. The further something travels, the longer it should take.

Easing

How a movement speeds up and slows down. Nothing in the real world starts or stops at a constant speed, so anything on screen that does looks wrong before you can say why. Easing is what separates movement that feels alive from movement that feels like a machine part.

Choosing motion

Do

  • Pick the job, not the numbers. The --sc-motion-transition-* recipes already carry the timing that suits each one.
  • Let distance scale duration: a small control settles in 50–150ms, a full-edge surface takes 300–500ms.
  • Use ease-out for entrances, ease-in for exits, and ease-in-out for anything visible before and after.
  • Keep travelling loops on linear. A curve on a repeat puts an acceleration and a deceleration at the seam, which reads as a stutter every iteration.
  • Give every property in a single gesture the same composite, so the parts start and finish together.
  • Animate translate, scale and opacity, because they composite on the GPU and never trigger layout.

Don't

  • Don't hardcode a duration or a curve. If a value isn't on the scale, the nearest stop is almost always right.
  • Don't put a spring or any overshooting curve on opacity or colour: an opacity that overshoots past 1 is a bug. They're spatial only.
  • Don't pair different composites across one gesture. A 100ms slide next to a 200ms fade stops moving halfway through and reads as unfinished.
  • Don't animate layout properties (width, top, margin) when a transform will do.
  • Don't set a setTimeout to match a CSS duration. The timer starts a frame before the animation does, so the element is removed while still visible. Listen for transitionend.
  • Don't reach for elastic or bounce in product UI; they read as toy-like on a control. Keep them for brand and illustration.

Accessibility

For some people motion isn't a matter of taste. Vestibular disorders can turn a screen-crossing animation into nausea, dizziness or a migraine, and the person on the receiving end has usually already told their operating system so.

When someone turns on reduced motion, every product duration drops to zero automatically. Components inherit that, shadow DOM included, so you never need to add your own opt-out.

The demos on this page follow the same rule. With reduced motion enabled at the OS level, everything above becomes instant, because the demos read the same tokens your components do.

Two deliberate exceptions, both because zeroing would remove meaning rather than calm it:

  • The loop durations (1000/5000) are never zeroed: a 0ms loop doesn't run at all, and a stopped spinner stops communicating “in progress”. The spinner instead slows to 2s and keeps turning.
  • A shimmer is large-area repeating movement and the most likely vestibular trigger in the set, so it degrades to a still gradient rather than a faster one.

Beyond the tokens: never convey information through motion alone, keep anything that loops out of the reading path, and avoid large-area parallax entirely.

Design handoff

Curve names are shared with Figma so a curve keeps its identity across tools. Two things don't travel, and are worth knowing before a handoff:

  • After Effects inverts the words. Its “Easy Ease In” decelerates into a keyframe, which is this system's ease-out. Everything else in the pipeline, Figma included, matches the names used here.
  • linear() curves don't round-trip. Springs, emphasized, elastic and bounce are sampled multi-point curves. Figma has no equivalent (its springs are parameterised by mass/stiffness/damping and its presets are unpublished), and in After Effects or Lottie they're a multi-keyframe construction rather than an ease. Match by eye and document the intent.

Durations sync across to Figma as number variables, but easings have nowhere to bind on a prototype transition, so they travel as documentation rather than as bound variables.

State change

Confirming a tap, a hover, a selection. The control stays exactly where it is and only its colour changes, because the user is already looking at it and moving it would be noise.

Fade

Something appears that was not there before, without claiming to have come from anywhere. A toast is the clearest case: it shows up where it will sit, says its piece and goes.

Enter and Exit

A surface with a home off-screen. It comes in from the edge it lives on and leaves the same way, so the user always knows where it went and where to find it again.

Scale

A dialog belongs to the whole screen rather than to one side of it, so it grows into the middle instead of sliding in from somewhere arbitrary.

In place

Content that was already there becoming more or less of itself.

Reposition moves an element that stays on screen the whole way.

Choreography

Motion across more than one element. A transition handles a single thing arriving or leaving; these handle two things trading places, or a group arriving together.

They're mixins rather than tokens because each one puts its elements on different sub-durations inside a single shared total, which no one custom property can hold. They aren't in the main stylesheet, so import them where you need them:

Stagger

A group arriving together, but not all at once. The eye gets a moment to take in each item instead of meeting the whole list at the same instant.

Siblings start 20ms apart, the interval Carbon measured as the smallest that meaningfully reduces effort. Past 25 children the interval compresses so the total never runs beyond 500ms.

The preview runs slower than that on purpose. Five rows at the real interval are done in 80ms, over before the eye reads it as a sequence.

Fade through

Switching between two places with no relationship to each other, like top-level nav destinations. Nothing slides, because there's no direction that would mean anything.

The two halves run in sequence rather than together: the old view is gone before the new one starts. Overlapping them would put two semi-transparent screens on top of each other, which reads as a glitch.

Shared axis

Moving between two places that are related: forward and back through a hierarchy, or side to side between peers. Both views travel the same axis, so the direction tells the user which way they went.

Three axes: x for lateral moves, y for vertical, and z for depth, where the outgoing view grows and recedes while the incoming one comes forward. Pass $direction: backward to reverse it.

Both views travel a short distance and cross-fade, which keeps them reading as two parts of one screen. When the new view is a whole screen of its own, use Push.

Push

One whole view replacing another, sliding edge to edge. It's what mobile platforms do between screens. Reach for it when the thing arriving is a place of its own, not a change inside the current one.

Nothing fades. Both views stay opaque for the whole move, so the change reads as replacement rather than a blend. Give the container overflow: hidden so the view that leaves is clipped at the edge.

Two axes: x and y, with $direction: backward to reverse it. There's no z, because push is lateral by definition. Use shared axis when you want depth.

Container transform

A card growing into the thing it opens, so the user keeps hold of what they tapped. The browser does the work through the View Transitions API: give both elements a matching name and it morphs the bounds, position and content between them.

The demo above runs the timing the mixin sets, but moves the card's bounds by hand. That's the argument for letting the browser do it: bounds are a layout property, and animating layout is the one thing this page tells you not to do.
